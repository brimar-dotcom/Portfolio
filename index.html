Overview

Los Alamos National Laboratory gave this project to an Environmental Testing of Structures research group at New Mexico State University to study the environment a flight vehicle experiences during a high-altitude drop.

The eventual goal is to test an aluminum flight vehicle at approximately 100,000 ft. Smaller PLA vehicles validate the system first, before the higher-risk test. The project combines simulation, laboratory vibration testing, and flight testing to better understand the conditions the vehicle experiences and to collect data that can validate the system.

My role

I am now the team lead of an eight-person group on the High Altitude Weather Cone (HAWC) project. I started on the project as an electronics team member, responsible for wiring, assembling, and testing the electronics for the parachute deployment system.

I tested three GPS modules and compared their altitude readings, the time each needed to get a satellite fix, and how well each held a signal enclosed inside the flight vehicle. I programmed and tested the GPS in Arduino/C++ and helped select a module with reliable performance.

The system uses a barometer for primary altitude, with GPS as a backup. It also includes a Teensy, NiCd batteries, an accelerometer, and electronics that fire a burn wire to release the parachute. I completed about three iterations of the electronics and wiring and helped prepare the system for the 1,000- and 2,000-ft drops. The deployment mechanism was successfully demonstrated.

Overview

As part of my work with a research group at New Mexico State University, under Los Alamos National Laboratory, I have been involved in experimental testing to study nonlinear dynamics and find the natural frequencies of different structures.

The work is part of a larger effort to understand how structures respond to different types of excitation and to compare physical testing with simulation. I have mainly worked with cone-shaped structures made from materials such as aluminum, plus some 3D-printed PLA structures. The experimental results are compared with ANSYS simulations to evaluate how closely the physical behavior matches the predicted behavior.

We also research coupling: how the modes change when a system like the cone is assembled piece by piece, instead of tested as one finished part.

My role

My role has focused on setting up and running the vibration tests and analyzing the resulting data. For the cone tests, I use accelerometers to measure the structural response while exciting the cone with a hammer. The testing software provides a frequency response function (FRF), which lets us identify the natural frequencies of the structure.

I have also worked on developing a free-free test setup, where the structure has to be supported without adding a fixed constraint that could change its natural response.

A significant part of this work has been troubleshooting. Wiring issues affected the collected data, and I had to find and correct them before getting reliable results. Testing is also slow, because the hammer must be used carefully to avoid unintended double impacts that interfere with the measurements.

The contest

WERC is a consortium at New Mexico State University that runs an annual Environmental Design Contest for undergraduate engineering teams. Each team picks a real environmental challenge, then designs and builds a working model of its solution. Industry, government and academia take part, and every field of engineering can compete.

Our task

For this capstone project, our team is working on Task 4, developed with the NASA Artemis Surface Systems Team. The challenge is a wireless charging system for a lunar terrain vehicle at the lunar south pole. Power has to cross a physical gap capacitively, with no electrical contact, and the system has to cope with extreme cold, abrasive charged lunar dust, rugged terrain and long dark periods.

As test and validation engineer, my part is the testing side: checking that the bench-scale prototype meets what the task asks for and that the results hold up against the full-scale design.

Requirements below come from the Task 4 Preview 2 document and may change as WERC releases updates.

werc.nmsu.edu

Bench-scale prototype must

  • Deliver at least 25 W of continuous DC power for 10 minutes
  • Reach at least 70% DC-to-DC efficiency
  • Keep a transmitter-to-receiver gap of at least 25 mm (1 in.)
  • Fit within 31 in. long, 11 in. wide and 11 in. high

Full-scale design targets

  • At least 1 kW of DC power to the vehicle
  • At least 80% DC-to-DC efficiency, with 90% as a goal
  • 100 mm nominal gap, working from 75 to 150 mm
  • Charging with up to 50 mm lateral and 10 degrees angular misalignment

Tests WERC will run at the contest

  • Low-temperature charging performance in a cooler
  • Alignment tolerance: lateral offset, angular misalignment and gap distance
  • Autonomous vehicle alignment under low-angle lighting
